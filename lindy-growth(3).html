the two findings
what I found in your docs, your pricing page, and a user review
finding 1: the trial closes before the product proves itself

Lindy already has the calibration surface. Drafting Instructions in settings lets users describe their tone, sentence length, and sign-offs. VIP email alerts let users name priority contacts. Both work for the user who finds them.

The problem is when they're surfaced. Settings pages get discovered by power users in week 2, after early drafts haven't yet matched their voice. New users on day 1 don't navigate to settings. They just text Lindy back.

The pricing page says it directly: "Lindy observes how you write emails, respond to messages, and handle your calendar over time. The more you use it, the better it gets." The trial is 7 days. The product's strongest moments require observation time that extends past the trial window.

Day 1 meeting prep draws from web research and LinkedIn lookups. Day 7 prep pulls past meeting history, prior email threads, and pattern recognition. That progression is the capability curve, and anyone on the team can verify it by comparing their own first and tenth meeting brief.

"There's a specific moment in the second week when Lindy caught something I would have completely missed."

Mejba Ahmed, two-week Lindy review, April 2026
finding 2: the referral loop is half-shipped

The JD calls out viral loops as a feature category to build. Lindy has the email half already shipped. It's turned off.

The "Sent from Lindy" signature in email drafting is off by default. The equivalent for calendar invites doesn't exist at all. Both are passive channels that expose the product to non-users every time the product is used, without requiring anyone to opt in or refer.

The email signature is off-by-default because "Sent from Lindy" implies AI authorship, which is a professional sensitivity. "Scheduled by Lindy" on a calendar invite is coordination metadata. It's closer to "Sent via Calendly" than "Written by AI." Different sensitivity profile.

experiment 1
move calibration from settings to the first conversation
the test

Half of new trial users get 4 quick questions from Lindy in their first iMessage conversation. The other half get the current experience. After 7 days, compare how many in each group converted to paid.

the 4 questions

  • "Paste 2-3 emails you've sent that sound most like you"
  • "Who are your 5 VIP contacts?"
  • "Any calendar blocks I should protect?"
  • "Formal or casual with clients?"

These aren't new features. Drafting Instructions and VIP alerts already exist in settings. The test moves them from a settings page to the first conversation, where the user is most willing and the personalization engine has the most to gain.

why this should work

Whatever the underlying mechanism (context accumulation, retrieval, or fine-tuning on user feedback), the input is volume of observation. Three pasted emails compress days of passive observation into seconds of explicit signal. If Lindy knows your voice and priorities from hour 1 instead of day 7, its outputs are better sooner, so you're more likely to pay.

primaryDay-7 trial conversion rate (variant vs control)
secondaryTime to first unedited draft — days until Lindy produces a draft the user sends without changes
secondaryDay-3 engagement rate — are users interacting more when early outputs are better?
guardrailSetup completion rate — does the extra 4 minutes suppress sign-ups?

Why this is counterintuitive: the 60-second setup optimizes for sign-up completion. Trial conversion is a different number. More setup friction leads to faster personalization, which leads to a faster value moment, which leads to higher conversion. Testable.

if it fails

A negative result at the aggregate level doesn't kill the hypothesis. It tells you where to look next. Segment the failing cohort: did certain user types still convert at higher rates? If founders converted but individual contributors didn't, the onboarding questions may be calibrated for one workflow but not another. If completion rate on the 4 questions was low, the problem is the prompts themselves, not the timing. If completion was high but conversion didn't move, the bottleneck isn't calibration speed — it might be trigger cadence (how often Lindy re-engages during the trial) or initial output quality independent of personalization.

Each failure mode points to a different next experiment. That's the value of running the test: even a loss narrows the search space.

experiment 2
turn on the referral loop
two-part test

part 1 — do new users want the email signature?

During onboarding, ask new trial users: "Want recipients to know Lindy helped draft this?" Measure how many turn it on when asked vs. how many currently find the toggle in settings on their own. Same feature, just surfaced earlier. Puts a number on whether the sensitivity concern holds for new users.

part 2 — add the calendar equivalent

Add a "Scheduled by Lindy" footer to calendar invites with a link to a landing page. Count how many non-users click through and start a trial.

  • Landing page at lindy.ai/invited (purpose-built, not the main homepage)
  • Copy: "Someone you know uses Lindy to manage their schedule. Try it free for 7 days."

Turn on one half, ship the other half, instrument both.

primaryTrial sign-ups attributed to /invited page + email signature combined
secondaryEmail signature opt-in rate when prompted during onboarding vs. baseline settings-page discovery
secondaryClick-through rate on each surface (email vs calendar)
guardrailCalendar footer opt-out rate — if high, the team's existing sensitivity concerns hold for this surface too

I don't have the meeting-frequency data to estimate a referral coefficient. Guessing at a number the team can check against a dashboard in 30 seconds would just be performing confidence. The instrumentation gives you the number you'd actually need to decide whether this loop is worth investing in: non-user exposure rate per active user per week, compared across both surfaces.

if it fails

Part 1 and Part 2 can fail independently, and each failure teaches something different. If email signature opt-in is low even when prompted, the team's original sensitivity concern was right and the channel isn't worth forcing. Move on. If calendar footer click-through is near zero, non-users may not notice footer text in calendar invites, and the exposure surface needs to be more visible (invite title vs. footer, for example). If click-through is healthy but trial starts are low, the /invited landing page isn't converting — that's a copy and targeting problem, not a channel problem.

The worst outcome isn't a failed test. It's never measuring the channel at all and continuing to guess whether built-in referral matters.
