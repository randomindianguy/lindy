Two of these loops have constraints worth addressing in the first 90 days.

lever 1
the activation timing collision

Lindy already has the calibration surface. Drafting Instructions in settings lets users describe their tone, sentence length, and sign-offs. VIP email alerts let users name priority contacts. Both work for the user who finds them.

The problem is when they're surfaced. Settings pages get discovered by power users in week 2, after early drafts haven't yet matched their voice. New users on day 1 don't navigate to settings. They just text Lindy back.

The pricing page says it directly: "Lindy observes how you write emails, respond to messages, and handle your calendar over time. The more you use it, the better it gets." The trial is 7 days. The product's strongest moments require observation time that extends past the trial window.

the mechanism

On day 1 the user is most willing to try Lindy and least equipped to direct it. They don't know what to ask for, and Lindy doesn't know how they write. Willingness decays daily. Capability climbs only as fast as Lindy can observe: each approved draft, each calendar preference, each ignored email teaches it something.

The point where the user knows what to delegate and Lindy delivers it in their voice lands somewhere in the second week. The 7-day trial closes before that happens.

Day 1 meeting prep draws from web research and LinkedIn lookups. Day 7 prep pulls past meeting history, prior email threads, and pattern recognition. That progression is the capability curve, and anyone on the team can verify it by comparing their own first and tenth meeting brief.

the experiment
hypothesis

Moving existing calibration from a settings page to the first hour of the iMessage conversation will compress the capability curve by front-loading data the engine would otherwise take days to observe passively. The user is most willing in that first hour, and the personalization engine has the most to gain.

what the variant looks like

After connecting email and calendar, users get 4 prompts via iMessage (not a settings page):

  • "Paste 2-3 emails you've sent that sound most like you" — demonstrative voice calibration, richer signal than the declarative Drafting Instructions text field
  • "Who are your 5 VIP contacts?" — relocates the VIP alert setup from settings to onboarding
  • "Any calendar blocks I should protect?" — scheduling rules
  • "Formal or casual with clients?" — tone segmentation

The wedge here isn't building new calibration. It's moving existing calibration to where it matters most: the first hour, before uncalibrated outputs wear down trust.

why this works mechanically

Whatever the underlying mechanism (context accumulation, retrieval, or fine-tuning on user feedback), the input is volume of observation. Three pasted emails compress days of passive observation into seconds of explicit signal. Five named VIP contacts give the priority model what it would otherwise take a week to infer from email frequency patterns.

The specific mechanism doesn't matter for the experiment design. The time compression does.

Why this is counterintuitive: the 60-second setup optimizes for sign-up completion. Trial conversion is a different number. More setup friction leads to faster personalization, which leads to a faster value moment, which leads to higher conversion. Testable.

lever 2
the viral loop that's already half-shipped

The JD calls out viral loops as a feature category to build. Lindy has the email half already shipped. It's turned off.

The "Sent from Lindy" signature in email drafting is off by default. The equivalent for calendar invites doesn't exist at all. Both are passive channels that expose the product to non-users every time the product is used, without requiring anyone to opt in or refer.

The email signature is off-by-default because "Sent from Lindy" implies AI authorship, which is a professional sensitivity. "Scheduled by Lindy" on a calendar invite is coordination metadata. It's closer to "Sent via Calendly" than "Written by AI." Different sensitivity profile.

the experiment
two-part test

part 1 — test appetite for the existing half

During trial onboarding, prompt new users directly: "Want recipients to know Lindy helped draft this? Turn on the Lindy signature." This measures whether new users opt in at higher rates when prompted during onboarding vs. discovering the toggle in settings later. Same UX, same toggle, lower political cost than flipping the default. Puts a number on whether the sensitivity concern holds for new users.

part 2 — ship the calendar half

  • Calendar invite footer: "Scheduled by Lindy — the AI assistant that runs your work life"
  • Link to lindy.ai/invited (purpose-built page, not the main homepage)
  • Landing page: "Someone you know uses Lindy to manage their schedule. Try it free for 7 days."

The work is to turn on one half, ship the other half, and instrument both.

primaryTrial sign-ups attributed to /invited page + email signature combined
secondaryEmail signature opt-in rate when prompted during onboarding vs. baseline settings-page discovery
secondaryClick-through rate on each surface (email vs calendar)
guardrailCalendar footer opt-out rate — if high, the team's existing sensitivity concerns hold for this surface too

I don't have the meeting-frequency data to estimate a referral coefficient. Guessing at a number the team can check against a dashboard in 30 seconds would just be performing confidence. The instrumentation gives you the number you'd actually need to decide whether this loop is worth investing in: non-user exposure rate per active user per week, compared across both surfaces.

90-day plan
intern scope → FT pipeline

month 1: instrument and diagnose

Week 1: Write the Lever 2 spec. Part 1: propose onboarding prompt that surfaces the existing email signature toggle to new trial users. Part 2: spec the calendar invite footer, /invited landing page, and UTM structure. Instrument the attribution events.

Week 2-3: Eng ships Part 1 (onboarding prompt, low effort) and begins Part 2 (new surface + landing page). Data starts collecting. In parallel: build the activation timing dashboard, define candidate activation metrics, segment trial users by which value moment they hit, when, and whether they converted.

Week 3-4: First read on email signature opt-in data. Identify the actual time-to-crossing for the 50th and 75th percentile trial user. Calendar footer goes live.

deliverable: activation timing dashboard + email signature opt-in data + calendar footer live

month 2: experiment

Week 5-6: Launch "Teach Lindy about you" A/B test (Lever 1). Control = current 60-second setup. Variant = 60-second setup + 5-minute structured onboarding via iMessage.

Week 6-7: First read on Lever 1. Iterate on variant based on early data — likely a second pass on which prompts compress observation time most efficiently. In parallel: analyze Lever 2 data across both surfaces. Compare opt-in/opt-out rates and attribution rates.

Week 7-8: Continue running Lever 1 to significance. Prepare dual-surface referral attribution report.

deliverable: one live experiment at or near significance + dual-surface attribution report

month 3: scale and handoff

Week 9-10: Read experiment results. Identify winning variants. Calculate impact on trial conversion and organic sign-ups.

Week 10-12: Build the handoff package for the FT Growth PM. The handoff is three things: a north star metric proposal, a growth levers map that decomposes it, and a prioritized experiment backlog with the first results attached.

deliverable: growth playbook + experiment database

The intern scope builds the measurement infrastructure and tests the first growth hypotheses. The FT role takes what works and scales it: expanding the activation flow, deciding whether the scheduling loop deserves a full investment, and running the next round of experiments.

why me
same shape, different domain

I've solved this shape of problem before. At Interview Kickstart, our AI courses had strong webinar demand but conversion to paying student was well below company average. Roughly $200K in lost revenue. I dug in and found it: trust was collapsing at the decision point because sales reps couldn't confidently explain course content. The obvious fix was sales training. I didn't want that. Training is a recurring tax. Every new hire, every new course, you're back to square one.

Instead I went upstream. Built expert-led video assets that delivered credibility independent of who was on the call. A/B tested with 250+ webinar registrants. The group that received expert videos converted at 14% higher rate. $200K in incremental revenue from a fix that didn't need to be re-applied every quarter.

Same shape here. Lindy's trial delivers uncalibrated outputs while it passively learns. That's the unconfident-rep equivalent. The fix isn't waiting longer. It's front-loading calibration into the first hour so the product sounds right from day 1.

The domain changes. The move is the same.
